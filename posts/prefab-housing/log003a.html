Who Owns
the Quality?

Why telling a factory to "just comply" doesn't work — and what the automotive industry figured out decades ago.

A practitioner's view on supply chain ownership in prefab construction.

Prefab Supply Chain Procurement Compliance Automotive

Same Supply Chain. Different Outcomes.

A luxury car and a counterfeit car part can come from the same country — sometimes the same region. The difference is never geography. It is who defined the specification, and who took responsibility for it.

This is the question that prefab construction in Australia has not yet answered.

What Happens in Prefab Procurement

In many prefab projects, the process starts like this.

A developer or builder contacts an overseas factory. They ask: what can you make? What materials do you use? Can you send us a list?

The moment you ask a supplier what they have, you have handed over control.

A factory's job is to produce at a price. If nobody specifies that the steel must be a certain grade, or that plumbing fittings must carry WaterMark certification, the factory will use what is available and cost-effective. That is not corner-cutting. That is a business operating within the brief it was given.

The brief was incomplete. That is the real problem.

Does Local Assembly Fix This?

Some argue that if the final assembly happens in Australia, the compliance problem is solved.

It isn't. Not fully.

Local assembly helps. It brings licensed trades into the process. It creates inspection points. It puts Australian practitioners on the ground at the final stage.

But the materials inside that assembly were decided earlier — at the design stage, during procurement, before the factory started production. If the steel came from a supplier that was never verified, local assembly does not change that. The components are already in the building.

I worked in automotive manufacturing before moving into the building industry — at a first-tier supplier, sitting directly below the vehicle manufacturer in the supply chain. Even with production happening locally, our QC team ran sampling checks on every incoming supplier part. We operated on JIT principles, feeding components directly upstream. And one of our products was sourced from Shanghai and shipped straight to the vehicle manufacturer without touching our floor.

The point is this: even in a tightly controlled, locally assembled product, the supply chain extended well beyond what you could see in the building. You managed it. You did not assume it.

When Australian manufacturing moved offshore — and it did, significantly, over the past few decades — the supply chain got longer. But the compliance responsibility did not move with it. Someone still has to own that chain from end to end. In construction prefab, that part has never been clearly resolved.

Who Should Own It?

In automotive, the answer is straightforward: the brand owns it.

Toyota does not make every component in a car. But Toyota defines every component. Their engineers work with suppliers from the beginning. They audit. They test. They do not leave material selection to the factory and check compliance at the end.

The brand owns the standard because the brand owns the product.

In prefab construction, the equivalent question is: who owns this product?

Often, the answer is unclear. The developer commissioned it. The builder procured it. The factory made it. The installer assembled it. Each party holds a piece of the process — but nobody holds the whole product.

When nobody owns it, compliance becomes something everyone assumes someone else is handling.

The Certifier's Role — and Its Limits

A building certifier or surveyor is not the answer to this problem.

Their role begins when the product arrives on site. They work within a defined scope. They rely on licensed trades to certify their own work. They are not procurement officers. They are not supplier auditors.

Asking a certifier to catch an upstream procurement failure is asking them to work outside their professional boundary — and outside the point in the process where anything can still be changed.

So What Is the Solution?

Honestly? No single practitioner can fix this.

Awareness helps. Specifying materials clearly at the design stage helps. Bringing local compliance knowledge into the process before procurement starts helps. These are things individuals and teams can do on their own projects today.

But the structural problem — that nobody clearly owns the prefab product from end to end — is not something one person or one project resolves.

Real change will likely come from the market, not from regulation alone. When a company with the scale and discipline of a Toyota or an IKEA enters the prefab housing space and builds a product designed for compliance from the ground up — specifications locked, supply chain audited, materials verified before production starts — that is when the industry standard shifts. Not before.

Until then, the most useful thing practitioners can do is be honest about where the gaps are. Stop assuming that an overseas factory understands the difference between Australian standards and those of any other market. Stop waiting until the product arrives at the wharf to ask whether it complies.

And stop blaming the construction method for a problem that was always about ownership.

Prefab is not the problem. Unclear ownership is.
