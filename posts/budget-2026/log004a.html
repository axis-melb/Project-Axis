Who Does the
2026 Budget
Actually Help?

A ground-level view on housing supply, investor behaviour, and the gap between policy assumptions and commercial reality.

Capital does not flow where it is punished. That is not a political statement. It is a commercial one.

Housing Policy 2026 Budget Negative Gearing CGT Supply Development

The Assumption Behind the Policy

The Albanese Government's 2026 Federal Budget has made housing affordability its centrepiece. The headline measure: reforms to negative gearing and Capital Gains Tax (CGT) designed to redirect investor capital away from established properties and toward new builds — with the stated goal of helping first home buyers enter the market.

The logic, on paper, is straightforward. Remove negative gearing for established homes. Push investors into new construction. Increase supply. Lower prices.

But commercial decisions are not made on paper. They are made on numbers. And from where I sit — working in construction and development day to day — there is a significant gap between what this policy assumes investors will do, and what the commercial reality of new construction actually looks like right now.

Capital Goes Where It Is Rewarded

The foundational assumption of this policy is that, once negative gearing is removed from established properties, investors will redirect their capital into new builds.

This assumes new builds are financially viable. At the moment, for many developers and investors, they are not.

Before a single brick is laid on a new residential development, the cost structure already includes town planning, council contributions, engineering consultants, surveyors, and other professional fees — tens of thousands of dollars before construction even begins. Commercial holding costs at current interest rates, accumulating every month through the approval process. Stamp duty on entry. GST on exit. And a level of finish — landscaping, driveways, fencing, and service connections — that the market expects, but which volume builders routinely exclude from their advertised base prices.

When you add these costs together, the break-even price for a developer is often higher than what the market expects to pay. The margin once absorbed by negative gearing and the CGT discount is now being compressed from both ends — rising costs on one side, reduced tax concessions on the other.

No rational investor commits capital to a guaranteed loss. Capital does not flow where it is punished. It finds somewhere else to go.

The question this policy does not answer is: where does that capital go next?

A Note on Negative Gearing and CGT

It is worth being precise here, because the public debate often conflates two separate things — and because developers and investors are not the same actor in this market.

For long-term investors — those who buy, hold, and eventually sell — CGT is the relevant lever. The 50% CGT discount applies to assets held over 12 months, and reducing that discount directly changes the exit calculation on the entire investment. For this group, the question shifts from "can I hold this?" to "what do I actually walk away with at the end?" If that number no longer works, the investment does not happen.

For developers — those who buy, build, and sell — the picture is different. Development profits are typically treated as ordinary income under trading stock rules, not as a capital gain. CGT discount changes affect this group less directly. What actually compresses development feasibility is the cost stack: financing at current interest rates through a lengthy approval process, council contributions, construction costs, and a market price ceiling that has not moved in proportion to those inputs.

While both groups drive supply—investors provide the capital and developers deliver the builds—they react to different signals. The CGT reform completely misses the mark. If investors walk away because a higher CGT shrinks their exit profit, they stop buying off-the-plan. Without those buyers, developers simply stop building.

Does This Help Young Buyers Save a Deposit?

The policy is framed as helping young Australians into the market. But consider what that path actually looks like today.

A graduate on a $100,000 salary takes home roughly $73,000 after tax — a figure anyone can verify on the ATO's income tax calculator. After rent, groceries, transport, and basic living costs in any major Australian city, the realistic saving capacity is a fraction of that. The path to a deposit on a median-priced property is still measured in years, not months.

CGT reform does not change that equation. It does not increase take-home pay. It does not reduce rent in the short term. It does not accelerate deposit saving.

What it may do, at the margin, is reduce competition for established properties. But a first home buyer competing with fewer investors for an existing home is not the same as a new home being built.

Shifting a property from an investor to a first home buyer is a change of ownership. It is not a change in supply.

The Lag Effect

In construction, supply is a lagging indicator. The projects completing today were financed, approved, and commenced one to two years ago. What you see finishing now reflects decisions made in a different market.

From where I sit, the enquiry level for new residential development — the early-stage conversations that eventually become building permits — has been thinning. This is an observation, not a data set. But in construction, the permit pipeline is the leading indicator. By the time reduced supply shows up in completion numbers, the decisions that caused it are already 18 to 24 months in the past.

If investors and developers are pausing now — and the commercial logic above suggests many are — the pipeline consequence will not be visible in today's housing data. It will show up later, when the projects that were never started do not finish.

What Would Actually Move the Needle

A healthy supply of new housing requires one thing above all: it has to be commercially viable to build.

That means reducing the approval timeline and the holding costs that accumulate during it. It means development contribution frameworks that do not price projects into unviability before construction starts. It means a tax structure that treats new residential construction as something worth incentivising.

Redirecting investor behaviour through tax reform is not the same as making new construction viable. The assumption that one automatically produces the other is where this policy, in my view, has a significant gap.

The housing shortage is a supply problem. Supply problems are solved by making it easier and more commercially viable to build — not by changing who owns the existing stock.
